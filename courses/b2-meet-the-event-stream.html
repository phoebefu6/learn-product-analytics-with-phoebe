Part 1 · covers the shape of a raw hour

What one hour looks like

5 min live

Before grouping anything, look at the raw shape: how many rows, which columns, and what mix of event types. GH Archive gives you 8 raw columns and 279,130 rows for this hour, and the mix is lopsided in a way most product streams share. One event type, PushEvent, is 56.2 percent of everything. Six types together cover more than nine tenths. The moment you see that, you know a naive "events per user" number is really "pushes per user" wearing a general name.

LiveFlatten first, then look3 min▶

Each line of the file is one nested JSON object: actor is itself a dict with an id and a login, repo another, payload a third whose keys depend on the type. pandas reads the file in one call, but a column of dicts cannot be grouped. The first move is to pull out the handful of scalar fields you will actually group by: a UTC timestamp, the type, the actor login and id, the repository name. Five flat columns. Everything else stays in the raw frame for when a question needs it.

Then three cheap looks before any metric: the row count, the column list, and value_counts on type. On this hour that already tells you the stream is a push stream with other things mixed in. Your own product's frame will have the same skew somewhere, and it is worth finding before someone builds a chart on "total events".

Self-studyWhere the hidden moments still hide2 min▶

The type mix hides the same trap session 1 met in the validator. CreateEvent is 14.2 percent of the hour, but on the tracking-plan pass only 10,384 of its 39,497 events were repository births; the rest were branches and tags. A bar chart by raw type therefore overstates "new projects" by a factor near four. When a type's share looks surprisingly large, open its payload and count the sub-kinds before believing the bar.

The org column is worth a glance too: it exists only when a repository belongs to an organisation, so it is missing on most rows. pandas will show it as NaN. That is a legitimate "does not apply", not a data-quality fault, and it is the kind of absence a plan should document per property.

Real world

The frame that would not group. A common pattern: an analyst loads a product export where every row's properties is a dict, tries groupby("properties"), gets an unhashable-type error, and spends an afternoon on it. The fix is always the same as tonight's step 2: decide which three to six scalar fields the questions need, flatten only those, and leave the nested column alone. A frame is for grouping; the raw JSON is for looking things up.

Part 2 · covers actors and bots

Who wrote the diary

6 min live

279,130 events came from 74,491 actors, and the split is nothing like even. The median actor has 1 event in the hour; the mean is 3.75; the maximum is 25,458. 52.1 percent of actors appear exactly once. The top 1 percent of actors, 744 accounts, produce 38.7 percent of the events. And 409 of the logins end in [bot], which is GitHub's own suffix for apps, and those 409 wrote 15.5 percent of the hour. The busiest login of all does not carry the suffix.

LiveThe bot rule is a decision, so write it down3 min▶

GitHub makes the easy part easy: apps act under logins ending in [bot], so str.endswith("[bot]") finds 409 actors and removes 15.5 percent of the hour in one line. The top five logins show both halves of the problem. Three are declared bots: github-actions[bot] 19,528 events, dependabot[bot] 8,962, renovate[bot] 6,769. One, SolukGece at 3,033, has no suffix and no obvious tell. And the busiest of all, censameesss at 25,458 events in sixty minutes, has no suffix either. Nobody types 25,458 actions in an hour. A login without the suffix can still be automation.

So the rule has two parts, and both belong in the code with a comment: the suffix rule, which is safe and exact, and a behaviour rule, which is a judgment. Whichever threshold you choose for the second part, print the count of actors and the share of events it removes on every run. A bot rule nobody can see is a metric nobody can audit.

Self-studyMedian 1, mean 3.75: which one goes in the deck3 min▶

Both, with labels, and neither alone. The mean of 3.75 is pulled up by a right tail so long that one account has 25,458 events, 25,458 times the median. The median of 1 says what a typical actor did, which is: one thing. The 52.1 percent who appear once are not a quality problem; they are what a public activity stream looks like in any single hour, since most people are not active in any given sixty minutes. The mistake is reporting "3.75 events per active user" as if it described a person. It describes a mixture of people and machines.

The top-1-percent figure is the honest summary of concentration: 744 accounts, 38.7 percent of events. When a leader asks how engaged "our users" are, this is the number that stops the conversation from being about an average that no single user resembles. Session 5 builds the full curve; tonight the one number is enough to change the question.

Real world

The monitor that became a power user. A common pattern: an uptime checker hits a product's login page every minute from a service account nobody flagged. In the weekly review it is the most engaged "customer" in the account, its sessions are perfectly regular, and it never churns. The fix is not smarter analytics; it is the sentence in the bot rule that says "service accounts are excluded, here is the list", reviewed when the list changes.

Part 3 · covers sessionizing

Where one visit ends

4 min live

Events are points; a visit is a stretch. The standard way to turn one into the other is an inactivity gap: sort each actor's events by time, and wherever the gap to the previous event is longer than a threshold, start a new session. Thirty minutes is the common default, inherited from web analytics, and it is a choice, not a law. On this hour, for the 74,082 human actors, the 30-minute rule yields 76,584 sessions, median 1 event per session, mean 3.08, and 2,502 actors who came back for a second session inside the same sixty minutes.

LiveThe tie a timestamp cannot break2 min▶

GH Archive records created_at to the second, and this hour has so many events that every single distinct timestamp is shared by 2 or more events. Sort by created_at alone and the order within a second is whatever pandas happened to leave it in, which is not stable and not meaningful. For a funnel, "did the push come before the pull request" can flip between two runs on the same data.

The fix is a second sort key that is unique and monotone. GitHub's event id is both, so sort_values(["actor_id", "ts", "id"]) gives a stable order. Your own product's stream needs the same thing: a sequence number, an ingest offset, or an id that increases. If it has none, say so in the definition, because any step-order claim is then partly decided by row order.

Self-studyThirty minutes is inherited, not derived2 min▶

The 30-minute gap comes from web analytics tooling and survived because it is a round number, not because anyone measured it for your product. For a developer pushing commits, a 45-minute think between pushes is one work session; for a mobile app opened for eight seconds, 30 minutes is an eternity. Test the threshold: compute session counts at 10, 30 and 60 minutes and look at how many actors gain a second session. On this hour 2,502 actors have 2 or more sessions under the 30-minute rule; that number is what a different gap would move first.

Then remember the window. One hour of data truncates every session that started before 15:00 or was still going at 16:00, so mean length is biased short and the "2+ sessions" count is biased low. When you sessionize a day or a week, the same thing happens at the edges of the extract, only less visibly.

Real world

The funnel that changed overnight. A common pattern: a step-order funnel is rebuilt on the same day's data and the conversion moves by a point. Nothing in the product changed. The events carried second-level timestamps, hundreds shared each second, and the sort had no tie-breaker, so a fraction of "viewed then clicked" pairs became "clicked then viewed" between runs. Adding the event id as a second sort key makes the number stop wandering; it does not make the order true, only stable, and the definition should say which.

★ Step 2 · a frame you can groupimport pandas as pd raw = pd.read_json("2024-03-04-15.json.gz", lines=True, compression="gzip") df = pd.DataFrame({ "ts": pd.to_datetime(raw["created_at"], utc=True), "type": raw["type"], "actor": raw["actor"].str["login"], "actor_id": raw["actor"].str["id"], "repo": raw["repo"].str["name"], }) print("events", len(df), "columns in the raw file", list(raw.columns)) # events 279130 columns in the raw file ['id', 'type', 'actor', 'repo', 'payload', 'public', 'created_at', 'org'] print("span", df.ts.min(), "to", df.ts.max()) # span ... one hour, 15:00 to 15:59 UTC on 2024-03-04 print((df["type"].value_counts(normalize=True) * 100).round(1).head(6).to_string()) # PushEvent 56.2 # CreateEvent 14.2 # PullRequestEvent 7.1 # DeleteEvent 5.4 # IssueCommentEvent 4.8 # WatchEvent 3.4

The bot rule, then events per actor. The suffix rule is one line. The distribution is one groupby. Print the median, the mean and the maximum together, always.

Sessionize the humans. Sort by actor and time, take the gap to the previous event within each actor, and start a new session where the gap is missing (first event) or over 30 minutes. A cumulative sum of the "new" flag numbers the sessions.

★ Step 5 · 30-minute gaphum = df[~df.is_bot].sort_values(["actor_id", "ts"]) gap = hum.groupby("actor_id")["ts"].diff() hum = hum.assign(new=(gap.isna() | (gap > pd.Timedelta(minutes=30)))) hum["session"] = hum.groupby("actor_id")["new"].cumsum() sess = hum.groupby(["actor_id", "session"]).agg(n=("type", "size"), start=("ts", "min"), end=("ts", "max")) print("human actors", hum.actor_id.nunique(), "sessions", len(sess), "median events/session %d, mean %.2f" % (sess.n.median(), sess.n.mean())) # human actors 74082 sessions 76584 median events/session 1, mean 3.08 print("actors with 2+ sessions inside the hour", int((hum.groupby("actor_id")["session"].max() >= 2).sum())) # actors with 2+ sessions inside the hour 2502

Three questions

Clear all three and analyst session 2 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · The [bot] suffix rule finds 409 actors and removes 15.5 percent of the hour's events. What does that share tell you about automation in the stream?

A rule by name is exact for the accounts that follow the convention and blind to the rest. Print what each half of the rule removes on every run, so the definition of "human activity" can be audited rather than believed.

2 · Why can created_at alone not order this stream?

A step-order claim such as "pushed before opening the pull request" needs a stable order. The id makes the order stable; whether it is the true order is a separate question the definition should state.

3 · What does the 30-minute gap decide in the sessionizer?

The threshold is inherited from web analytics, not derived from your product. Test 10, 30 and 60 minutes, pick one for a reason, and remember that a bounded extract truncates sessions at both edges.
